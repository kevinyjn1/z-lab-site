Matter in Extremes, Machine for Extremes

The Z Lab's research can be summarized into two words: Matter and Machine. In the Matter domain, we synergistically integrate statistical physics, molecular simulations, artificial intelligence, and neutron scattering experiments to extend our understanding of rare/extreme events and long timescale phenomena in complex materials. Particular emphasis is given to the physics and chemistry of liquids, glasses, and complex fluids, especially at interfaces, under extreme conditions, or when driven away from equilibrium. Concurrently, on the Machine front, we build robots for extreme environments, including swarm robots, wheel-leg hybrid robots, and soft robots. These two research areas, spanning from fundamental to applied, serve as integral pillars in their overarching mission to foster a sustainable, resilient, and secure energy infrastructure.

1.1Matter

1.1.1Physics and chemistry of liquids, glasses, and complex fluids, especially under interfacial/extreme/non-equilibrium conditions (water, metallic liquids, molten salts, ionic liquids, electrolyte solutions)

The water that never froze

Ordinary water freezes long before it can be cooled far enough to test a prediction from simulations: that below its famous density maximum, liquid water's density should stop falling and turn upward again. Confining heavy water in silica pores only 15 Å wide keeps it liquid down to 160 K, and a neutron diffraction peak then reads out its density. It passes through a minimum at 210 K, the first experimental report of one (Liu et al., Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. U.S.A. 2007).

Ordinary water in the same pores does the same at 200 K (Liu et al., J. Chem. Phys. 2013), while in water-repelling carbon pores the minimum is absent (Zhang et al., J. Phys. Chem. B 2009; Liu et al., J. Chem. Phys. 2015).

Fragile to strong

Neutrons can isolate the one layer of water coating a biomolecule. On DNA it slows along a curved, fragile law on cooling, then switches abruptly at 222 K to a straight, strong one, near the temperature where the DNA's own motion changes (Chen et al., J. Chem. Phys. 2006). Water on lysozyme and RNA makes the same switch at nearly the same temperature (Chen et al., J. Spectrosc. 2010).

The early papers read this as water driving the biomolecule; a later perspective qualifies that (Bertrand et al., Phys. Chem. Chem. Phys. 2013). On lysozyme a second crossover, at 345 K, coincides with the start of reversible unfolding (Zhang et al., J. Chem. Phys. 2009).

Where the straight line ends

Diffusion in a hot liquid follows an Arrhenius law, a straight line here. In the molten glass former LM601, neutrons find it falling below that line under about 1300 K, as atoms begin to move cooperatively (Jaiswal et al., J. Phys. Chem. B 2016). That is about twice the glass transition temperature and above the melting point. Simulations of a similar alloy show clusters of similarly mobile atoms growing faster below it (Jaiswal et al., Phys. Rev. B 2015).

In eleven metallic liquids the onset sits near twice the glass transition temperature; in molecular liquids, near 1.4 times (Jaiswal et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 2016).

A pinch of fission product in a reactor salt

What do fission products do to a molten reactor salt? Neutron measurements on FLiNaK show about 1 mol % of cerium or zirconium slows its ions and raises the diffusion barrier, while cesium changes little (Rakib et al., ACS Appl. Energy Mater. 2025). The authors' reading: highly charged ions bind fluoride into heavy complexes. Diffraction finds about 7.5 fluorides around each cerium (Rakib et al., J. Nucl. Mater. 2025).

In simulations of molten LiF-NaF, thorium fuel also gathers fluoride into clusters and lowers the heat conduction (Grizzi et al., J. Phys. Chem. B 2024; Grizzi et al., Phys. Rev. Mater. 2026).

Softer when charged

A flow battery stores more energy when its active molecules are more concentrated, but a crowded electrolyte turns viscous and stops conducting. In one such fluid, a phenothiazine in acetonitrile, the conductivity peaks near 0.7 mol/L and then falls as the viscosity climbs.

The lab's measurements and simulations show the discharged fluid stiffening sooner than the charged one. Its small lithium ions lose their solvent shell and bind the anions tightly, while the bulky charged molecule binds them only weakly (Farag et al., Phys. Chem. Chem. Phys. 2023).

From 100 atoms to 100,000

First-principles simulations of a liquid are limited to fewer than about 200 atoms and about 100 picoseconds, too little for a converged viscosity (Grizzi et al., Phys. Rev. Mater. 2026). The lab trains neural networks on such calculations so that their accuracy carries over to far larger boxes and longer times. For the reactor salt FLiNaK, the network reproduced first-principles forces about two orders of magnitude better than a classical model and ran about 2140 times faster than the first-principles calculation (Lee et al., J. Phys. Chem. B 2021).

Later potentials followed zinc chloride for nanoseconds (Lee and Z, J. Chem. Phys. 2024), held for compositions of LiF-NaF never seen in training (Murg et al., J. Appl. Phys. 2025), and ran 100,000 atoms of a thorium-bearing salt (Grizzi et al., Phys. Rev. Mater. 2026).

Including the EXPANSE and CHESS spectrometers for the Second Target Station of the Spallation Neutron Source.

Two clocks in a glass-forming salt

Neutron spin echo times how a ripple in a liquid's density fades. In CKN, a molten nitrate salt that forms a glass, it fades in two steps: a fast one within a picosecond, then a slow, stretched one that lengthens from 17 picoseconds at 519 K to 6 nanoseconds at 402 K. Both follow the liquid's structure, and below about 2.6 Å the slow step obeys different rules (Luo et al., Nature Commun. 2022; Luo et al., J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 2025).

EXPANSE, a wide-angle spin echo spectrometer planned for Oak Ridge's Second Target Station, would record many length scales and times at once; the lab is among the authors of its design paper (Do et al., Rev. Sci. Instrum. 2022).

1.1.4Rare/extreme events and long timescale phenomena in complex materials

Flood the landscape

Many changes in matter are rare because an energy barrier must first be crossed. To chart the barriers, the lab and its collaborators flood an energy landscape from the bottom up and record the energy at which each valley, loop, and void appears and disappears. The resulting barcode holds everything the usual tree of minima and barriers holds, plus the extra pathways it misses, and for alkane chains of any length it can be written down exactly (Mirth et al., J. Chem. Phys. 2021).

The same rising sea later turned variables learned by a neural network into molecular motions one can watch (Lee and Z, J. Chem. Phys. 2024).

1.2.1Robots in extreme environments

The swarm, wheel-leg, and soft robots below are all built for this. The student team names mission-critical work in the energy sector and homeland security as its immediate focus (Atombot on the Multidisciplinary Design Program site).

1.2.2Swarm robots and collective intelligence

Lose two, keep going

A swarm is many simple, cheap robots that together do what one robot cannot. In a seminar, the lab's principal investigator, Y Z, describes small units driven by vibration motors instead of wheels to keep the cost low, and a swarm that, like an ant colony, is not affected by losing one or two (Michigan Robotics seminar, 2025).

The student team describes its miniature swarm system as a platform for studying emergent behavior in many-robot systems (Atombot on the Multidisciplinary Design Program site).

1.2.3Wheel-leg hybrid robots

Wheels and legs in one machine

Wheel-leg hybrid robots are one of the lab's research themes: wheels cover flat ground quickly and cheaply, and legs take over where wheels cannot go. The lab works with a Unitree Go2-W, a commercial quadruped with a driven wheel at the end of each leg.

1.2.4Soft robots and human-compatible machines

Be the controller: steer the underwater arm

The lab's underwater soft arm is a stack of identical, mostly 3D printed modules. Each is bent by two water-filled silicone bellows and carries its own valves, pressure sensors, and circuit board (Null et al., RoboSoft 2023). It is hard to steer: its response is nonlinear and depends on its history, and its only commands are eight valves, open or shut.

A model predictive controller, tuned automatically and entirely offline from one recorded data set, ended inside 39 of 40 goal squares, about 3 mm from the target on average, ahead of four other controllers trained on the same data (Null et al., IEEE Robot. Autom. Lett. 2024).

Soft is not linear

A soft robot's silicone can stretch to several times its length, and its stress does not rise in a straight line with stretch, so linear material models fail long before the working range. The lab added a hyperelastic (neo-Hookean) material to the open-source MOOSE framework and released it as Kraken. It predicted the pressure needed to extend a real silicone bellows to within 1.4 kPa on average (Wandke and Z, IEEE Access 2021).

Extended to motion, Kraken reproduced the bounce frequency of three silicone bellows to within about 3%; in full 3D swinging it overestimates frequencies by 10 to 30% on average (Wandke and Z, IEEE Access 2023).

Atombot

Atombot is the lab's student robotics team: about 50 undergraduate, master's, and PhD students, working with the Multidisciplinary Design Program. It runs two projects, Hydrogen and Helium, and building hardware is the main focus.
